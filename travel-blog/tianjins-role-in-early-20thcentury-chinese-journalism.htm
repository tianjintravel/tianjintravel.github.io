Tianjin’s Role in Early 20th-Century Chinese Journalism

  • Home
  • Travel Blog
  • -Blog Details

When you think of early 20th-century Chinese journalism, your mind probably jumps straight to Shanghai. The Bund, the foreign concessions, the roaring presses of Shen Bao and Xinwen Bao—it all feels like the epicenter of China’s media revolution. But if you’re a history buff or a curious traveler with a passion for media, you’re missing half the story if you skip Tianjin. This port city, just a stone’s throw from Beijing, was not just a second fiddle to Shanghai. It was a crucible where modern Chinese journalism was forged, a place where foreign missionaries, reformist intellectuals, and revolutionary firebrands all jostled for space in the pages of dozens of newspapers and magazines. And today, you can still walk the streets, visit the old printing houses, and feel the ghost of that vibrant, chaotic, and transformative era.

The Concession City: Why Tianjin Became a Media Hub

Let’s start with geography and politics. Tianjin in the early 1900s was a city carved up by foreign powers. The British, French, Japanese, Germans, Italians, Russians, and Austro-Hungarians all had their own concessions, each with its own legal system, police force, and, crucially, its own censorship rules. For a journalist in Qing-dynasty China, where the imperial government could shut down a paper and throw you in jail for criticizing the court, the foreign concessions were a godsend. You could publish what you wanted, as long as you didn’t offend the concession’s ruling power. And since the concessions competed with each other for influence, they often turned a blind eye to political commentary that targeted Beijing or other foreign powers.

This created a unique ecosystem. Tianjin became a safe haven for exiled intellectuals, radical students, and even secret revolutionary societies. The city’s proximity to Beijing—just a two-hour train ride even in those days—meant that news from the capital could be reported, analyzed, and distributed faster than anywhere else. And because Tianjin was a major port, imported printing presses, typefaces, and paper supplies arrived regularly, making it one of the most technologically advanced cities for publishing in all of China.

The Missionaries Who Started It All: The Peking and Tientsin Times

If you visit Tianjin today, you can still see the remnants of the old British Concession, especially around the Wudadao (Five Great Avenues) area. But to understand the roots of Tianjin journalism, you need to go back to 1894, when a British missionary named John Charles Oswald started publishing The Peking and Tientsin Times. This was one of the first English-language newspapers in northern China, and it set the tone for what would follow. Oswald wasn’t just reporting news; he was using the paper to advocate for educational reform, Western medicine, and Christian values. But more importantly, he trained a generation of Chinese editors and typesetters who would go on to launch their own publications.

You can still find copies of The Peking and Tientsin Times in the Tianjin Library, and if you’re lucky, you might stumble upon a reprint in one of the antique bookshops near the Ancient Culture Street (Gu Wenhua Jie). The paper’s old office building, located near the Haihe River, has been converted into a boutique hotel, but the facade still bears the original brickwork. It’s a perfect spot for a selfie with history.

The Reformist Wave: Liang Qichao and the Shiwu Bao

Now, let’s talk about the big names. Liang Qichao, the towering intellectual of the late Qing, spent a significant amount of time in Tianjin after the failure of the Hundred Days’ Reform in 1898. He fled to Japan, but he maintained close ties with Tianjin’s reformist circles. His newspaper, Shiwu Bao (The Chinese Progress), was actually founded in Shanghai, but its influence in Tianjin was immense. Why? Because Tianjin was the gateway for reformist ideas to reach Beijing. Copies of Shiwu Bao were smuggled into the capital by the thousands, read aloud in teahouses, and passed from hand to hand among students and officials.

But Liang’s real impact on Tianjin journalism came through his later work. In 1902, he launched Xinmin Congbao (The New Citizen) from Yokohama, but the paper’s distribution network in Tianjin was so sophisticated that it effectively operated as a local publication. Tianjin’s bookstores and newspaper vendors would have fresh issues within days of publication. Today, you can visit the Liang Qichao故居 (Liang Qichao’s Former Residence) on Minyuan Road in the Italian Concession. It’s a charming two-story villa that has been turned into a museum. Inside, you’ll see his study, his printing press, and even a replica of his desk. It’s a must-see for anyone interested in the intersection of journalism and political reform.

The Revolutionary Underground: The Tianjin Youth and the May Fourth Movement

Fast forward to the 1910s and 1920s, and Tianjin became a hotbed of revolutionary journalism. The May Fourth Movement of 1919, which started as a student protest in Beijing against the Treaty of Versailles, quickly spread to Tianjin. And it was in Tianjin that some of the most radical student newspapers were born. One of the most famous was Tianjin Xuesheng Lianhehui Bao (Tianjin Student Union Newspaper), which was later renamed Tianjin Qingnian (Tianjin Youth).

The editor of Tianjin Youth was none other than Zhou Enlai, the future premier of the People’s Republic of China. Yes, that Zhou Enlai. He was only 21 years old at the time, but he was already a skilled writer and organizer. He used the paper to call for national independence, social justice, and an end to foreign imperialism. The paper was published from a small room in the Nankai School, which still exists today as Nankai University. You can visit the Nankai University campus and see the plaque commemorating Zhou’s old office. It’s a quiet, leafy spot, but if you close your eyes, you can almost hear the clatter of the typewriter and the murmur of student activists planning their next issue.

But Tianjin Youth wasn’t the only game in town. There were dozens of smaller papers, some lasting only a few issues, others running for years. They were printed on cheap paper, distributed by hand, and often shut down by the authorities within weeks. But they kept coming back, like weeds through concrete. For a traveler, the best way to experience this underground journalism scene is to visit the Tianjin Museum of Modern History, which has a dedicated gallery on the May Fourth Movement. They have original copies of these newspapers, along with photographs of the young editors. It’s a humbling reminder of how much courage it took to put words on paper in those days.

The Commercial Press: The Dagong Bao and the Rise of Professional Journalism

While the revolutionaries were printing their manifestos, a more commercial, professional style of journalism was also emerging in Tianjin. And the poster child for this movement was The Dagong Bao (L’Impartial), which was founded in 1902 but really came into its own in the 1910s and 1920s. The Dagong Bao was different from the partisan papers of the era. It prided itself on its independence, its investigative reporting, and its commitment to factual accuracy. It was the New York Times of northern China, and it set the standard for modern Chinese journalism.

The paper’s founder, Ying Lianzhi, was a Catholic convert who believed that journalism should serve the public good, not just political factions. He hired some of the best writers of the day, including Wu Zhihui and Zhang Jiluan, and he invested heavily in modern printing technology. The paper’s offices were in the French Concession, on a street that is now called Jiefang Bei Lu. The building is still there, a handsome Art Deco structure that has been restored and now houses a museum dedicated to the history of The Dagong Bao. You can walk through the old newsroom, see the original printing presses, and even read some of the paper’s most famous front pages.

What makes The Dagong Bao so fascinating for a modern traveler is its global perspective. The paper had correspondents in London, Paris, Tokyo, and New York. It covered world events with a depth that was rare for Chinese newspapers at the time. And it wasn’t afraid to criticize the government, whether it was the warlords of the 1920s or the Nationalist regime of the 1930s. This independence came at a cost: the paper was shut down multiple times, and its editors were frequently arrested. But it always came back, and its legacy lives on in the Chinese press today.

The Printing Presses of Tianjin: A Walking Tour

If you’re a hands-on traveler who wants to see where the actual printing happened, Tianjin has a few hidden gems. Start at the Tianjin Printing Museum, which is located in the old Japanese Concession. This museum is a bit off the beaten path, but it’s worth the trip. It houses a collection of vintage printing presses, including a rare hand-operated press that was used to print some of the earliest Chinese newspapers. You can even try your hand at typesetting, using the old lead typefaces.

Next, head to the Italian Concession, which has become a trendy area with cafes and boutiques. But tucked away on one of the side streets is the old headquarters of the Yishibao (The Social Welfare), a newspaper that was founded by the Catholic Church in 1915. The building is now a restaurant, but the owners have preserved the original facade and some of the interior details. If you ask nicely, they might show you the basement, where the printing presses used to be.

Finally, don’t miss the Haihe River waterfront, where many of the old newspaper distribution offices were located. In the early 20th century, this area was a hive of activity, with newsboys shouting the day’s headlines and horse-drawn carts loaded with bundles of papers. Today, it’s a peaceful promenade, but if you look closely at the old buildings, you can still see the faded signs of long-gone newspapers. It’s a perfect place for a contemplative walk, especially at sunset.

The Women of Tianjin Journalism: Unsung Heroes

No discussion of Tianjin’s journalism history would be complete without mentioning the women who broke into a male-dominated field. One of the most remarkable was Lü Bicheng, a poet and journalist who worked for The Dagong Bao in the 1910s. She was one of the first female editors in China, and she used her position to advocate for women’s education and suffrage. She also wrote some of the most beautiful poetry of the era, which you can still find in anthologies today.

Another trailblazer was Shi Liang, a lawyer and journalist who co-founded the Nüzi Yuekan (Women’s Monthly) in Tianjin in the 1920s. This magazine was a platform for feminist ideas, covering everything from marriage reform to workplace rights. It was published from a small office in the British Concession, and it had a circulation of just a few thousand. But its impact was enormous, inspiring a generation of Chinese women to demand equality.

For a traveler, the best way to honor these women is to visit the Tianjin Women’s Museum, which has a section on female journalists. You can see photographs of Lü Bicheng and Shi Liang, along with copies of their publications. It’s a small museum, but it’s filled with powerful stories.

The Legacy: Why Tianjin Still Matters

So, why should a modern traveler care about all this? Because Tianjin’s role in early 20th-century journalism is not just a historical footnote. It’s a story about the power of the press to shape a nation. The newspapers that were printed in Tianjin—whether they were reformist, revolutionary, or commercial—helped to create a public sphere in China, a space where ideas could be debated, governments could be criticized, and citizens could inform themselves. This was the foundation of modern Chinese democracy, and it’s a story that is still relevant today.

Moreover, Tianjin itself is a city that wears its history on its sleeve. You can’t walk down a street without stumbling upon a building that was once a newspaper office, a printing house, or a journalist’s home. The city has done an excellent job of preserving these sites, and many of them are open to the public. So, if you’re planning a trip to China, don’t just go to Beijing and Shanghai. Take the high-speed train to Tianjin, spend a few days exploring its journalism heritage, and you’ll come away with a deeper understanding of how China’s modern media was born.

And who knows? Maybe you’ll even feel inspired to start your own newspaper. Stranger things have happened in Tianjin.

Practical Tips for the Traveling Journalism Enthusiast

  • Best time to visit: Spring (April to June) and autumn (September to November) are ideal, with mild weather and fewer tourists.
  • Getting around: The Tianjin Metro is efficient and cheap. Most of the historical sites are within walking distance of each other in the old concessions.
  • Language barrier: English is not widely spoken outside of tourist areas, so download a translation app or hire a local guide who specializes in history.
  • Must-read before you go: The Search for Modern China by Jonathan Spence has an excellent chapter on the early Chinese press. Also, check out The Birth of Chinese Journalism by Barbara Mittler for a deeper dive.
  • Souvenir idea: Look for replica front pages of old Tianjin newspapers in the antique shops on Ancient Culture Street. They make great conversation starters.

Tianjin is a city that rewards the curious traveler. It’s not as flashy as Shanghai or as historic as Beijing, but it has a quiet, stubborn charm that lingers long after you’ve left. And for anyone who loves the written word, it’s a pilgrimage site. So pack your bags, grab a notebook, and get ready to walk in the footsteps of the journalists who changed China.
